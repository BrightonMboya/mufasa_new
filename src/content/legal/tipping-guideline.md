Tipping is a common question when it comes to Tanzania safaris, Kilimanjaro treks, and holidays generally — how much, and whether it's necessary at all after a significant trip spend.

## Tipping culture in Tanzania

Tanzania's wage structure makes tipping a traditional practice. Service workers and guides rely on gratuities as customary compensation, with the expectation that visitors will acknowledge their contribution.

## Why it matters

Guides, cooks, and porters depend substantially on tips to supplement modest salaries. King Mufasa Expeditions pays above-market wages, but tips remain crucial to worker support — and they motivate quality personnel and show appreciation for exceptional service.

## Getting tips to the right people

Direct hand-delivery to individual staff members ensures proper distribution. Alternatively, an envelope or a pooled group tip works, provided the crew understands the arrangement clearly.

## When to tip

The end of the safari or trek is the natural moment, once you've had the chance to properly judge the service.

## Why tips aren't built into the price

King Mufasa Expeditions keeps tipping separate from package pricing on purpose. Tipping in Tanzania is tax-free; folding it into the trip price would make it subject to an 18% VAT, and would subject the workers themselves to income tax — reducing what they actually take home.

## Who to tip

- Guides
- Cooks
- Drivers
- Servers

## Kilimanjaro tipping

Unlike safari gratuities, which function more as a performance bonus, Kilimanjaro tipping is closer to a necessary wage supplement. Daily minimum wages on the mountain run: porters ($10), guides ($20), assistant guides/cooks ($15).

A typical group includes a head guide, assistant guide, cook, porters, and — depending on the package — toilet porters and an assistant cook.

### Daily group tipping guide

| Role | Daily tip |
| --- | --- |
| Head guide | $20–25 |
| Assistant guide | $12–20 |
| Cook | $12–20 |
| Assistant cook | $10–15 |
| Porter | $7–10 |
